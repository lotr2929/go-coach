## liberty
A liberty is an empty point directly beside a stone: up, down, left or right. A stone or group with no liberties is captured. Say "show liberties" and I will mark them on the board.

## atari
Atari means a stone or group has one liberty left and will be captured next move unless it escapes or you capture something nearby. Say "show liberties" and the last liberty turns red.

## capture
You capture stones by filling their last liberty. They are removed and counted as prisoners. Prisoners do not count in area scoring, but removing stones changes who controls the points.

## group
Stones of one colour that touch along a line form a group. They share liberties and live or die together.

## eye
An eye is an empty point surrounded by your own stones. A group with two separate eyes can never be captured, because the opponent cannot play in either eye: each would be suicide.

## alive
A group is alive if it cannot be captured, usually because it has two eyes. A dead group will be captured eventually, even if it is still on the board at the end. At scoring you tap dead groups to remove them.

## ko
Ko is a shape where two players could capture one stone back and forth for ever. You may not recreate the previous position, so play elsewhere first, ideally a move the opponent must answer (a "ko threat"), then retake.

## suicide
A move that leaves your own stone or group with no liberties is illegal, unless it captures. The captured stones give it new liberties.

## territory
Territory is empty space that only one player surrounds. In area scoring, your score is your stones plus your territory, and White adds komi. An empty region counts for a side only if it touches only that side's stones. The dark and light squares on the board are an estimate, not a count: ask me "what are the squares?" to see how they are made.

## scoring
We use area scoring: stones plus surrounded empty points, with White adding komi. When both players pass, dead stones are removed and the totals are compared.

## komi
Komi is compensation for White moving second. Here it is 6.5 on 9×9 and 13×13, 7.5 on 19×19, and 0.5 with handicap stones. The half point means no draws.

## handicap
Handicap stones let a weaker player take Black with extra stones already placed. White then moves first and komi drops to 0.5.

## pass
Passing means skipping your turn. When both players pass in a row the game ends and is scored. Pass when nothing useful is left.

## ladder
A ladder is a running chase: a group in atari extends, is put in atari again, and so on in a zig-zag. It works for the attacker unless the runner meets a friendly stone (a ladder breaker).

## snapback
A snapback is a sacrifice: you let the opponent capture one stone, then recapture a larger group because their capturing stone is itself in atari.

## seki
Seki is a standoff where neither side can capture the other without being captured first. Both groups stay on the board and the shared space scores for nobody.

## sente
A move in sente forces a reply, so you keep the initiative. A move in gote does not: the opponent can ignore it and play the biggest move elsewhere.

## tenuki
Tenuki means ignoring the opponent's last move and playing elsewhere.

## star-points
Star points are the dots on the board. They mark good opening points and where handicap stones go.

## lines
The first line is the edge. The third and fourth lines balance territory and influence, which is why most openings use them.

## opening
In the opening, take the corners first, then the sides, then the centre: corners make territory with the fewest stones. Keep stones connected and check for atari every move.

## connect
Connecting joins your stones into one group that shares liberties; cutting separates the opponent's stones so each part is weaker. Most fights are about one or the other.

## kifu
Kifu (Japanese for "game record") is the written record of a game: every move in order, so the game can be replayed. Go Coach has 100 famous kifu to study: type "famous games" to open them.

## joseki
A joseki is a well-known sequence of moves in a corner that experts agree is fair to both sides. Learn the ideas behind them rather than memorising them: a joseki that does not suit the rest of the board can still be a poor choice.

## rank
Go players are ranked in kyu and dan. Beginners start at about 30 kyu, and the number goes down as they improve, to 1 kyu. After 1 kyu comes 1 dan, and dan numbers go up as players get stronger. Professionals have their own dan ranks.

## hane
A hane is a move that bends around the end of the opponent's stones, diagonally from your own stone. It blocks their path and is one of the most common moves in a fight.

## goban
The goban is the Go board. A full board has 19×19 lines; beginners often start on 9×9 or 13×13. Stones are played on the points where the lines cross, not in the squares.

## tsumego
Tsumego are Go problems: a small position where you must find the move that kills or saves a group. Solving them is one of the best ways to get stronger.

## moyo
A moyo is a large framework: an area you have started to surround but not yet secured. It may become territory, but the opponent can still try to invade or reduce it.

## influence
Influence (or thickness) is the power of strong stones facing the centre. They are not territory yet, but they help you attack, fight and build territory later.

## aji
Aji (Japanese for "taste") means hidden possibilities left in a position, such as a weakness the opponent might use later. Bad aji means you have left something behind that could cause trouble.

## nigiri
Nigiri is how players decide who takes Black. One player takes a handful of white stones, the other guesses odd or even by showing one or two black stones.

## byoyomi
Byoyomi is overtime. When a player's main time runs out, they must play each move within a set period, such as 30 seconds, or lose on time.
