## first-move
Start in a corner, then the sides, then the centre: corners make territory with the fewest stones. Ask me to "explain the first move in more detail" for the full reasoning, or say "hint" for a suggestion in this position.

## first-move-detail
Why the first move usually goes in a corner:
1. A corner stone has two edges to help it, so it takes fewer stones to claim territory there than anywhere else.
2. The sides come next. The centre is the hardest area to hold, so it is usually left until later.
3. Which point? On a 9×9 board the favourite points are the 3-3 points (C3, G3, C7, G7): they are safe and claim a corner quickly. The centre point (E5) is also played, but it is harder to use. On 13×13 and 19×19 the star points (the dots) near the corners do the same job.
4. Avoid the first line (the edge) and usually the second line: a stone there has few liberties and little room for territory.
5. After your move your opponent replies. You can then take another corner, or press the stone that came near yours.

## squares
The squares on the board are an estimate of who will own each empty point. Switch them on with the Territory tab under the board.
1. I play many quick random games from the current position to the end and score each one. The Accuracy slider in Settings sets how long I may spend on this (1 to 60 seconds), so a longer time means more games and a steadier map.
2. For every empty point I note how often it ended up Black's or White's. A dark square means Black usually got it, a light square means White did, and a bigger, stronger square means the result was more consistent. Hover over a point to see its score, from 0 (an even split) to 1 (the same side owned it in every game); the colour shows which side. Squares appear once a score reaches 0.15.
3. In a real score, an empty region counts for a side only if it touches only that side's stones.
4. Early in the game the squares are mostly noise, and more so on 13×13 and 19×19. Trust them more in the middle of a game, and most of all on 9×9.

## rules
The rules in brief:
1. Black plays first; players alternate placing one stone on an intersection.
2. A stone or group with no liberties (empty neighbouring points) is captured.
3. You may not play a stone with no liberties unless it captures.
4. Ko: you may not recreate the previous position.
5. When both players pass, the game ends. Score = stones plus surrounded empty points; White adds komi.

## more.liberty
Liberties are the lifeblood of a group. Count them by looking at the empty points next to every stone of the group: a group on the edge has fewer, so it is easier to capture. Fighting in Go is mostly a race to take away the opponent's liberties while keeping your own.

## more.atari
Atari is a warning, not yet a capture. The player in atari can extend (add a stone that brings new liberties), capture a neighbouring stone, or give the group up. Think before you put an enemy group in atari: if it can simply escape, you may only have made it stronger.

## more.ko
A ko fight is a contest over who can force the other to answer elsewhere. A move that threatens something bigger elsewhere is a ko threat: if the opponent answers it, you may retake the ko. The player with more useful threats usually wins the ko.

## more.eye
One eye is not enough. The opponent fills the outside liberties until your group is in atari, then plays into the eye, which is legal because it captures. With two separate eyes the opponent can never play in either, so the group lives. Making two eyes is the central idea of life and death.

## more.komi
Black moves first, which is worth roughly 6 to 7 points on a normal board, so White receives komi as compensation. The half point (6.5 or 7.5) means a draw is impossible.
