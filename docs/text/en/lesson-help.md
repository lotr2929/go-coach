# Help inside a lesson

What the coach says when someone asks for more detail during a lesson ("explain", "I don't understand"),
one answer per lesson, followed by its other replies inside lessons. The lessons themselves come later.

How to edit: change the words under a "## name" heading, but leave the heading itself as it is.
Words in curly brackets are filled in by the app: keep them.

## board
Each stone sits on a crossing of two lines, so a 9×9 board has 81 points. Black always moves first. Stones stay where they are put unless they are captured, and a player may pass instead of playing.

## liberties
Count liberties by looking up, down, left and right of a stone. A stone in the middle has 4, on an edge 3, in a corner 2. Connected stones share their liberties, and the number matters because a group with none is captured.

## capture
To capture, fill the last empty point beside the enemy stone. The white stone already has black stones on three sides, so the one green dot is the capture point.

## groups
Two stones that touch along a line are one group. The white pair shares its liberties, and only one is left, at the bottom. Fill it and the whole group comes off the board at once.

## atari
The black stone has white stones on three sides and one liberty left. If you do nothing, White plays there and captures it. Extending to that point joins your new stone to the old one and gives the group more liberties, so it is no longer in atari.

## suicide
At A all four neighbours are white stones, so a black stone there would have no liberties. It would also capture nothing, because each white stone still has liberties of its own. A move like that is suicide and is not allowed.

## exception
This time the white stone at the top has black stones on three sides, so A is its last liberty. Playing at A fills that liberty and captures the stone first. Once it is removed, your new stone has a liberty, so the move is legal.

## ko
In the starting position Black can capture the white stone by playing A. That leaves one black stone with a single liberty, and White could capture it straight back at B, then Black could capture again, for ever. The ko rule stops that: White must play somewhere else first.

## eyes
A and B are two separate empty points, each completely surrounded by black stones. If White plays at A, the stone has no liberties, and Black still has the other eye at B, so Black is not captured and the white move is suicide. The same is true at B. White could only fill an eye if the other one were already gone, which is why two eyes make a group safe.

## scoring
Each side scores its stones plus the empty points that only it surrounds. Black has 9 stones and the 27 empty points on its left; White has 9 stones and the 36 points on its right, so White is ahead by 9 before komi.

## strategy
Corners are efficient because two edges help your stones, sides come next, and the centre is hardest to hold. Early on, play on the third and fourth lines.

## connect
Stones are connected only if they touch along a line, up, down, left or right. Two stones that meet at a corner (diagonally) can be cut: White plays at A or B and splits them into two weaker groups. Playing at A or B joins your stones into one group that shares its liberties.

## cut
The two black stones touch only at a corner, so the points A and B are both cutting points. A white stone on either one stands between them. Black can still connect with the other point, but the cut forces Black to spend a move on it, and that is how cuts win tempo and make groups weak.

## tiger
A tiger’s mouth is three stones around one empty point, with the fourth side open. A stone played inside has only one liberty, so it is captured at once. That is why the tiger’s mouth counts as connected without filling the point, and why it is lighter and covers more ground than a solid connection.

## triangle
An empty triangle is three stones in an L with the fourth corner empty. Compare it with three stones spread out: the triangle has fewer liberties and surrounds less. That is why it is called bad shape. It is sometimes the only move, but look for a better one first.

## race
In a capturing race, count each side’s liberties. Each move fills one. If one side has more liberties, it usually wins; with equal liberties, whoever moves first wins. Liberties shared by both groups, and eyes, change the count, which later lessons can cover.

## border
A gap in a wall lets the empty points on your side join up with ground that touches the opponent, and ground that touches both colours counts for nobody. One stone in the gap seals it, and the whole area becomes your territory.

## dame
A neutral point, or dame, is an empty point between the two sides that touches both colours. It counts for nobody. At the end of the game, fill the neutral points before you pass: in area scoring, each one you fill is a point for you.

## ladder
In a ladder, Black gives atari again and again while White runs in a zig-zag. It works if the chase reaches the edge, and fails if it meets a White stone on the way (a ladder breaker). Always follow it to the end in your head before you start.

## net
A net blocks the escape route instead of giving atari. The white stone has two liberties; whichever one it uses to run, the net stone is waiting to close it in. Look one point further than the obvious atari.

## ending
A player may pass instead of playing. Two passes in a row end the game. Before counting, dead stones (stones that could not survive) are removed, then each side adds its stones to the empty points only it surrounds. White also adds komi.

## handicap
Handicap stones go on the star points, so a weaker player can take Black with an advantage. White then moves first and komi is reduced to half a point. On a 9×9 board two stones go on opposite corners, three add a third corner, and so on up to five.
