# A history of Go

Go is called weiqi in China, igo in Japan and baduk in Korea. It was invented in China more than 2,500 years ago, and is often described as the oldest board game still played today [1]. This page follows its story from ancient China to the computer programs of the twenty-first century.

## Origins in China

Nobody knows exactly how or why Go began. One story says generals used stones to map out battles. Others link the equipment to divination or to flood control, though the evidence for these ideas is thin [2].

The earliest written mention is usually taken to be the Zuo Zhuan, a chronicle from about the fourth century BCE that refers to an event dated 548 BCE [1][2]. A reference work on play says that chronicle was compiled no later than 389 BCE [8].

The oldest Go board found in China is a broken pottery board from the Western Han dynasty (206 BCE to 24 CE), dug up in Shaanxi Province [2]. Early boards had fewer lines than today's; archaeology shows a 17 × 17 grid in earlier centuries [1]. The earliest known 19 × 19 board is a ceramic one from the Sui dynasty (581 to 618), found at Anyang in Henan Province [2]. The first book about Go, the Dunhuang Go Manual of about the sixth century CE, already describes a 19 × 19 board [2].

In ancient China Go was seen as the refined pastime of scholars. It was counted as one of the four essential arts of the cultured scholar, alongside music, calligraphy and painting [1][2].

## Korea and Japan

Go reached Korea (where it is called baduk) by the fifth century CE and Japan (where it is called igo) by about the seventh. By then the 19 × 19 board had become standard [1]. The sources differ on the details: one tradition credits Kibi no Makibi, a scholar who had studied in Tang China, with bringing the game to Japan at the start of the eighth century [2].

In Japan the game was played actively in the Nara period (710 to 794) and became a favourite pastime of the aristocracy in the Heian period (794 to 1185) [2]. A reference work on play adds that Go flourished in Japan and became woven into its art and literature, and in modern times into manga and anime [8].

## The Go houses of the Edo period

Go made its most significant development in Japan during the Edo period (1603 to 1868) [5].

In 1612 the Tokugawa shogunate began to support four hereditary schools, called houses, to teach and play the game: Hon'inbō, Hayashi, Inoue and Yasui [2][3][4]. The first of them, Hon'inbō, was founded by Hon'inbō Sansa, a Buddhist monk whom the first Tokugawa shogun, Ieyasu, appointed as the minister of Go [3]. The heads of the houses received a salary from the shogunate [4], and they competed in official "castle games" (oshirogo), played at Edo Castle in the shogun's presence [3][5].

One of the most famous players of the era was Hon'inbō Shūsaku (1829 to 1862). He was heir to the Hon'inbō house but died of cholera before officially becoming its head [3].

## The Nihon Ki-in

After the end of the shogunate the houses lasted into the twentieth century, but they struggled without government backing [7]. In July 1924 the Nihon Ki-in (the Japan Go Association) was founded, and professional players came to belong to it [4][6].

## Go comes to the West

Western writers described Go early: Matteo Ricci in 1615 and Thomas Hyde in 1694. But the game did not become popular in the West until the late nineteenth century [2].

The turning point was Oscar Korschelt, a German engineer who learned the game from Hon'inbō Shūhō while working in Japan between 1878 and 1886. He published a detailed article on Go in 1880 and a book a few years later [2][9].

Because Korschelt learned the game in Japan, Go terms in Western languages come from Japanese, and Westerners usually say "go" rather than the Chinese "weiqi" [2]. The name Go is a short form of the Japanese word igo [1].

By the early twentieth century the game had spread through the German and Austro-Hungarian empires. Edward Lasker learned it in Berlin in 1905, and later helped to spread it in the United States, where the New York Go Club was founded and the American Go Association followed in 1935. The German Go Association was founded in 1937 [2]. The Second World War stopped most Go activity, but the game continued to spread afterwards [2].

## Computers and AlphaGo

Go was long seen as a grand challenge for artificial intelligence. The huge number of possible positions defeated standard methods, and for decades the best programs only reached the level of human amateurs [10].

That changed with AlphaGo, a program from Google DeepMind. In October 2015 it beat the European champion Fan Hui by five games to nothing, in the first match between an AI system and a professional player. The method was published in the journal Nature in January 2016 [10][11]. In March 2016 in Seoul it beat Lee Sedol, a winner of 18 world titles, by four games to one, in a match watched by over 200 million people [10].

Two moves from that match are famous. AlphaGo's move 37 in game two had about a one in 10,000 chance of being chosen by a human player. Lee Sedol's move 78 in game four, nicknamed "God's Touch", was just as unlikely, and it helped him win the one game he took [10].

In October 2017 DeepMind described AlphaGo Zero, which learned from games against itself alone, without human games. After three days of this it beat the version that had defeated Lee Sedol by 100 games to 0 [11]. Players around the world have studied the programs' moves ever since [10], and many now play on online servers such as KGS and IGS [8].
